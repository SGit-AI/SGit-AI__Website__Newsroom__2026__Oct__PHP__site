# What is the financial exposure, what does downtime cost against breach cost, and will insurance respond?

Q
The answer in thirty seconds

The documented cost so far is precaution, not breach: hours to a day of downtime for organisations that pulled sites, and engineering time to update. CISA listed both flaws as known exploited on 26 October, which raises the bar for 'we had not patched'. As of 09:43 UTC on 27 October, the latest capture in the evidence vault, no named organisation has said it was compromised through this flaw. Ask your broker now, in writing, whether a precautionary shutdown with no breach is covered. Do not use 70% of the Internet in any forecast.

[Read the site as Quinn →](../../explained/index.html)

The financial question has two sides and the evidence is lopsided: downtime is documented and measurable; breach is claimed and unconfirmed. Insurers are already treating this as an aggregation event.

## What to decide or do
- Cost of acting: operators that pulled sites report hours to a day offline; one operator served 94% of its page views from cache through a 13-hour shutdown. Engineering time to patch, restart and verify is the main cost for most.
- Cost of not acting: an unauthenticated code-execution flaw in the web tier; what it reaches is whatever that tier can reach, which is a question for the CISO's inventory, not for a percentage.
- Insurance: Insurance Business reports underwriters watching for an aggregation event and asks whether business-interruption cover responds to a precautionary shutdown with no breach; that depends on wording and waiting periods. Ask the broker now, in writing.
- Figures you will hear and should not use in a forecast: 70% of the Internet (a share of websites restated), 30 million websites (no basis), 22.6 million systems (method not given). Use your own inventory count.
- No named organisation has confirmed a compromise as of the latest capture. That is a statement about evidence, not about safety.

## What is still open
- Who first leaked the CVE IDs on 20 October, and where. The PHP Foundation does not say; a PHP security team member points to an anonymous Hacker News post, and Notebookcheck and Nguoi Quan Sat repeat that account. No copy of the post is in the vault.
- Whether the deleted Calif video is the one now embedded on Calif's own write-up. Calif's page does not mention the leak.
- Whether any site was actually compromised before it was patched or taken offline. Every claim of exploitation so far is of attempts, probes or honeypot hits, or, in CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities listing of 26 October, of exploitation with no victim named; the named cases do not state php2shell as the cause. Authorities in Singapore and Luxembourg now ask operators to look for traces; the answer will come from those searches, if it comes.
- Which sites were really taken offline, and why. The list now draws on more captured reports (Germany's hospitals and hosts, Morocco, Romania, Japan), but most are press accounts; few come from the organisations themselves (the Dutch government, Latvia's agency, Weird Gloop, the Charité's spokesman as quoted). A community tracker counts 51 listed and 50 back.
- Whether the 8.5.12 fix is complete. A Fediverse report of a third bug or incomplete fix reached oss-security on 21 October; PHP reportedly told Canonical it is unrelated. On 23 October PHP published an informational advisory for a separate flaw, CVE-2026-115560, in the session upload progress feature, reported by Cloudflare, SOCRadar and The Hacker News: it affects fully patched releases including 8.5.12, needs a rare session setting, will be fixed in the next regular release, and has session.upload_progress.enabled=0 as the mitigation; SOCRadar reports no exploitation and no severity score. PHP's advisory is not in the evidence vault, and whether this is the bug the Fediverse report described is not stated.
